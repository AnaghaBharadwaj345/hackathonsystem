Policy comparison

Runs a full 24-hour day through each policy on the same seed, so the only thing that changes is the scheduling decision.

What the MEDFLOW scheduler does. Every simulated minute it re-sorts everyone waiting into three tiers. Resuscitation and emergent cases sit above everything and are never deferred. Below them, anyone past their hard clinical deadline is served most-overdue-first — that is the starvation guarantee, and it is why level 4 and 5 patients stop walking out. Everyone else is ranked by acuity stretched by how far past target they have waited and by modelled deterioration risk. Resources are then acquired as whole bundles, atomically: a patient is admitted only when the bed, the clinician and the nurses are free at the same instant, which removes hold-and-wait and makes deadlock impossible. When the top patient still cannot be placed, the resource they are short of is reserved instead of being handed down the queue, and someone lower is backfilled into it only if they will be finished before the reservation comes due. A critical patient who needs intensive care and finds no ICU bed is boarded in a ward bed rather than left waiting.

Why the comparison is fair. Arrivals run on a separate random stream from in-hospital events, so all three policies face the identical patient sequence on a given seed and the only difference is the ordering decision. Waiting times include people who left without being seen, otherwise a policy that abandons its long waiters would look artificially fast.

Read the trade-off honestly. MEDFLOW does not win every column. Against strict triage it treats more people, roughly halves walkouts and cuts avoidable deterioration, and it pays for that with slightly longer average waits for level 2 and 3 patients, because overdue patients are now consuming capacity instead of being skipped. Push the load slider up and all three policies fail together — no scheduler manufactures staff that are not on shift.
